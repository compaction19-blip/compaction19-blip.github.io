Introduction

It is not unusual to put an AI-calculated task duration into a schedule or use code it has explained at work. But counting all printed sheets as good sheets changes the task duration. Likewise, guessing what a function does from its name alone can lead to an answer that differs from the code's actual output.

In this experiment, GPT, Claude, and Gemini all answered the three questions correctly. Each received the same questions for 1 attempt, with 0 retries. They tied at 3/3 each. What this result demonstrates is the accuracy of these particular answers. It does not establish that any model is better at other tasks.

The point is to go one step beyond noting that all three AIs gave the same answers. A person should be able to retrace the steps and verify that those answers satisfy the conditions in the questions.

Experiment Design

The 2026-10-26 record compares GPT-6.1 Sol High, Claude Opus 5.5 Medium, and Gemini 4 Argon. The results below are from the 3rd set in the record.

The test consisted of three questions, each checking something different.

  • The time needed to finish producing 50 good printouts.
  • The count of numbers from 1 to 100 that satisfy both the multiple condition and the digit-exclusion condition.
  • The output after applying Python's strip and replace in sequence.

In this setup, keeping track of every condition matters more than the calculations themselves. For the printing problem, you need to check what is being counted; for the multiples problem, how the conditions are grouped; and for the code, what each function actually does.

The scoring criteria are fixed in advance. In order, the answer key is 83 seconds, 15 numbers, ['c', 'a']. Changing the criteria after seeing the models' answers could lead to scoring the same expression differently. That is why we distinguish units from formatting and check whether the substance of each answer matches the key.

Why These Results?

This record alone cannot establish how the three AIs reasoned internally. What the questions do show is which conditions must be followed to reach the correct answers.

In the first problem, you must distinguish the total number of printed sheets from the number of good sheets. Since every 8th sheet is defective, printing 56 sheets produces 7 defective sheets and 49 good sheets. The next sheet, the 57th, is good and brings the total to the target of 50 good sheets.

Printing therefore takes 57 seconds. Cleaning after the 7 defective sheets takes 14 seconds. Organizing the batches after 10, 20, 30, and 40 good sheets happens four times, taking 12 seconds. The total is 57 + 14 + 12 = 83 seconds. The sentence stating that there is no organizing step after the final 50 good sheets determines where the calculation ends.

The second problem requires applying the multiple condition and the digit-exclusion condition together. The criterion used in this record is to select numbers that are “multiples of 4 or multiples of 6,” then exclude those containing the digits 4 or 6. A number that satisfies both multiple conditions is counted only once.

The numbers that pass these conditions are as follows.

8, 12, 18, 20, 28, 30, 32, 52, 72, 78, 80, 88, 90, 92, 100

There are 15 in total. If it is unclear what “and also” applies to, putting parentheses around the entire multiple condition when writing the question makes the meaning clearer.

In the third problem, strip("abrd") is not a command that finds and deletes the string "abrd" as a single unit. It removes characters from both ends for as long as it encounters a, b, r, or d. With "abracadabra", stripping the ends leaves 'c'.

"banana".replace("a", "b", 2) replaces only the first two occurrences of a, producing "bbnbna". Applying strip("bn") to this removes b and n from both ends, leaving 'a'. Checking the order of the function calls and the number of replacements separately lets you trace the final list.

Conclusion

Today, all three AIs answered correctly. At work, too, it helps to check the intermediate calculation states and the code's actual behavior rather than stopping at the fact that the answers agree.

  • Write down the target quantity and the total quantity processed separately.
  • Check whether a pause or organizing step also applies at the end.
  • When “or” and “and also” appear together, clarify the conditions with parentheses.
  • For short pieces of code, write down the intermediate strings or run the code directly to check the result.

These results record three questions presented to each model once, with no retries. Because the number of questions is small, it is difficult to use this as a basis for broader judgments about overall model performance or the value of paid services.

Original Experiment Prompt and Actual Answers

I sent the following questions to each of the three AIs exactly once.

Answer the following 3 questions briefly, by number.
1. You want to produce 50 good printouts using a printer. Printing 1 sheet takes 1 second. However, every 8th sheet the machine prints (the 8th, 16th, 24th... output sheets) is defective and must be discarded (it does not count toward the number of good printouts). Each time a defective sheet is printed, the machine immediately cleans its nozzles for 2 seconds. Also, every time 10 more good printouts accumulate (immediately after reaching 10, 20, 30, and 40 good printouts), it spends 3 seconds organizing the batch (there is no organizing step after the final 50 good printouts are complete). How many seconds in total does it take until all 50 good printouts have been produced? Give only the answer, briefly.
2. Among the natural numbers from 1 to 100, how many are multiples of 4 or multiples of 6 and also contain neither the digit '4' nor the digit '6'? Give only the answer, briefly.
3. In Python 3, what list is printed when print(["abracadabra".strip("abrd"), "banana".replace("a", "b", 2).strip("bn")]) is executed? Give only the answer, briefly.
