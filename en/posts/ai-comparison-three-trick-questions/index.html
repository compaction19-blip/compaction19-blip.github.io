Day 9 or Day 10? A 3-Question Challenge

Introduction

When transferring an AI-calculated schedule or code output into a work document, it is easy to trust a short, clear answer. But missing just one condition can move a deadline a day earlier or inflate a count by one. If you copy only the answer, you copy the mistake along with it.

In this experiment, conducted on 2026-10-22, GPT and Claude each answered all 3 questions correctly. Gemini answered 1 correctly and 2 incorrectly. Each of the three AIs received the same questions once, with no retries.

The takeaway is not a clean sweep by one particular AI. GPT and Claude tied for first place. The more useful observation is how answers that overlooked conditions came about, and where a person could have checked to catch those errors.

Experiment Design

There were three questions: the day a frog escapes a well, the count of natural numbers satisfying several conditions, and the output of Python code. Each question is short, but all require keeping earlier conditions in mind through the later steps.

The first question involves a well 26m deep. A frog climbs 7m during the day and slips down 4m at night. On days that are multiples of 3, however, it climbs only 5m during the day. The second question asks how many numbers from 1 to 100 contain the digit 5 after excluding multiples of 2 or 3.

The third asks for the output of the following code.

it = iter([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
print(2 in it, 2 in it, sum(it))

The correct answers, in order, are 10, 9, and True False 0. Establishing the correct answers first prevents us from marking an answer correct simply because multiple AIs agree. The grading criteria are the problem's conditions and execution order, not a majority vote.

The models used were GPT-6.1 Sol High in Work mode, Claude Opus 5.5 at Medium (중간) in an Incognito (시크릿) session, and Gemini 4 Argon in Temporary Chat (임시 채팅). They received the same questions with instructions to give brief, numbered answers.

Why These Results Occurred

The key to the frog question is that its daytime climbing distance varies by day. Simply adding the net distance after each night can also cause you to overlook whether it escapes during the day first.

It is easier to check by separating each day into daytime and nighttime. The frog is at 7m on the night of day 3, 14m on the night of day 6, and 20m on the night of day 8. Since day 9 is a multiple of 3, it climbs only 5m. Its highest point that day is 25m, still short of 26m. After slipping down to 21m at night, it reaches 28m during day 10.

In its follow-up explanation, Gemini said its mistake was adding the usual 7m on day 9. However, that explanation still needed checking. It used 19m as the starting height on day 9 and wrote 19+5=24m, but applying the conditions gives a starting height of 20m and a daytime height of 25m. An explanation of why an answer was wrong also needs to be checked independently.

The second question contains a trap: two overlapping lists. The following 7 numbers end in 5 and are not multiples of 2 or 3.

  • 5, 25, 35, 55, 65, 85, 95

The numbers with 5 in the tens place that satisfy the same conditions are 53, 55, 59. Adding the lengths of the two lists gives 10, but 55 appears in both. Subtracting it once gives the correct answer of 9. In its follow-up explanation, Gemini also said it had failed to subtract this duplicate. However, this is the AI's own explanation, so it cannot be treated as definitive evidence of the actual cause.

In the third question, an iterator's state changes as it is used repeatedly. An iterator is like a bookmark that does not automatically return to a previously read position. The first 2 in it reads 1 and 2 and evaluates to True. The second check reads through the remaining 3, 4, 5 but does not find 2, so it evaluates to False. At the end, no values remain, so the sum is 0.

The output is therefore True False 0. All three AIs got it right, but understanding the answer requires checking what remains after each test.

Conclusion

You can apply the same checking methods on a small scale when verifying AI answers at work.

  • Reapply day-specific exceptions on the relevant day.
  • Check whether the target has been reached at intermediate steps, too.
  • Look for overlapping items when combining multiple lists.
  • For code, track the remaining values and state in execution order.
  • Recalculate explanations of errors against the original conditions.

This experiment consisted of 1 set of 3 questions, with the same questions asked once each and 0 retries. GPT and Claude's tie for first place applies to this set; it does not establish a general ranking of AI performance.

Original Prompt and Actual Answers

I sent the questions below to all three AIs, 1 time each.

Give brief, numbered answers to the following 3 questions.
1. A frog is at the bottom of a well 26m deep. It climbs 7m during the day and slips down 4m at night. However, on days that are multiples of 3 (days 3, 6, 9...), it gets tired and climbs only 5m instead of 7m during the day. On which day does the frog first reach the top of the well (26m or higher)? Answer with a number only.
2. Among the natural numbers from 1 to 100, how many are neither multiples of 2 nor multiples of 3 but contain the digit '5' at least once? Answer with a number only.
3. What is the output of the following Python code? Give only the exact output on a single line.

it = iter([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
print(2 in it, 2 in it, sum(it))
